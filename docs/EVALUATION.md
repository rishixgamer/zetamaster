# Evaluate Training Effectiveness, Not Engagement

## Hypothesis

Given equal total practice time, focused adaptive training should lead to larger improvements in unseen standard-format arithmetic scores than ordinary mixed practice. This is a hypothesis, not a proven outcome.

## Individual use protocol

1. Collect 3–5 baseline standard-format runs across multiple days; record rolling median.
2. Train 10–20 minutes/day with targeted techniques and drills, with reasonable rest; log activity.
3. Benchmark 2–3 times/week under similar conditions. Avoid counting obvious aborted runs.
4. Compare rolling medians, 90th percentile question times, and weakest families over time.
5. Avoid asserting gains from isolated personal bests; assess independent mixed-operation transfer.
6. Define success as 8 of latest 10 standard runs ≥70.

## Multi-user experiment

- Randomize users into adaptive vs standard-mixed practice groups, matched total practice minutes.
- Use held-out seeded but equally distributed standard tests. Avoid training on these sequences.
- Record baseline ability, device/input type, practice frequency and dropout.
- Predefine the primary endpoint: median standard score change after N sessions.
- Report effect size, uncertainty interval and attrition; don't change the success metric after inspecting results.
- Monitor harm/overuse; don't use guilt-based streak features.

## Statistical safeguards

- First-input time is not identical to thought time.
- Very rare patterns must be shrunk or marked insufficient evidence.
- A tiny sample cannot yield a trustworthy 'mastery probability'.
- A performance plateau can reflect fatigue, noise, focus changes or a real training ceiling.
- Never market numerical score forecasts as calibrated until validated prospectively.
